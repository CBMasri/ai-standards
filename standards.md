# AI Standards

AI is a powerful tool, and I'd encourage everyone to use it. It can help us work faster, explore more ideas, solve problems we might otherwise get stuck on, and spend more of our time on the things that require human expertise.

As we adapt to this new way of working, it's important that we don't lose sight of the things that make us good at what we do. Using AI should make you more capable, but not less thoughtful, less accountable, or less human in how we work with each other.

When you use AI, these are the principles I'd encourage you to uphold.

## Own the output.

AI can help you produce work, but it does not share responsibility for the result. When AI contributes to something you create, that work is yours; you are accountable for its accuracy, quality, and whether it is appropriate for the context in which it is used.

In practice, this means:

- **Code:** Review and test it before handing it off to others. AI-generated code can contain bugs, security issues, or logic errors that look correct at a glance.
- **Reports and announcements:** Apply judgment to tone and substance. AI may not know your audience, your relationships, or the full context of a situation. An AI-drafted report may be technically fine but miss the mark entirely.
- **Research and analysis:** Verify facts and figures. AI can be wrong or oversimplify. When using it to summarize or analyze source material, check the result against the original.

Don't put your name on something you haven't actually reviewed. This applies to AI-generated work the same as anything else.

## Think for yourself.

People often call AI an “amplifier” because it has a tendency to magnify the effort, judgment, and understanding you bring to it. Of course, this cuts both ways: if you bring very little, it can amplify that too. When used effectively, it can compress days or weeks of work into minutes or hours. Used carelessly, it lets you move faster than your own understanding, and you end up with work you can’t explain or defend.

It’s often tempting to look at the first thing an AI generates and assume it’s good enough. This is rarely the case. It’s your responsibility to take the result and apply critical thinking. Look closely at the parts you don't understand, and challenge the response until it holds up under scrutiny or gives you a good reason to change your mind. Knowing what to ask, when to dig deeper, and when to push back takes practice, and developing these skills is part of learning to use AI well.

Writing a proposal, design document, or explanation forces you to develop an idea and work through the parts that aren’t yet clear. When you use AI to jump straight to the end result, you can easily skip the thinking that would normally happen along the way.

As the cost of producing work drops, the effort you put into it is what sets it apart. You cannot prompt caring, or your ability to defend why you made a choice. You only have those if you do the work. AI is not a replacement for judgment.

Don’t let your AI use make you less curious, less independent, or less capable.

## Keep conversations human.

There's a difference between using AI to produce work and using AI to talk to people. Documentation, reports, and announcements are work products. Use AI to draft and improve them as much as you like, as long as you review and own the output.

Direct conversation is different. When a teammate messages you on Slack, emails you, or asks for your feedback, they expect to be talking to you. Conversations are how we build relationships, and they only work if there's effort being made on both sides. Replacing your side of the conversation with generated replies undermines that trust. It’s hard to care when it feels like nobody else does.

Put simply:

- When you're talking directly with someone, the words should be yours. It's fine to use AI to think through a problem, check your grammar, or polish something you wrote. It's not fine to paste an AI's response as your own, even if you’ve asked it to imitate your voice or “make it sound less like AI”.
- If you use AI to help review a teammate’s work, assess its findings yourself and write your own feedback. Don’t have an agent post review comments on your behalf.
- If English isn't your first language, AI is a great tool for polishing your writing, and we encourage it. Keep your own voice, though. Your teammates would rather read your imperfect words than an AI's perfect ones.

If a conversation is worth having, it’s worth having yourself.

## Do your part before asking for theirs.

AI has made it much easier to produce content, but it hasn’t reduced the effort it takes someone else to read and understand it. Copying and pasting output you haven’t processed yourself shifts that burden to everyone else, often asking more of their attention than you’ve given it.

A good rule of thumb: what you are willing to write is generally the upper limit of what others are willing to read. You’re being paid for your expertise and insights, so understand what you’re sharing, trim it down to what’s important, and tell us what you think.

When you generate something with AI, you are the only one who sees the prompts and the constraints. Sending AI-generated material to a teammate can be useful, but you need to pull out the relevant findings and add your own context first. The other person should know why you’re sending it, what you think, and where you need their input, rather than having to work that out from a pasted response. Labeling it as AI-generated is a useful courtesy, though what matters more is showing that you’ve put thought into what you’re asking someone else to spend their time on.

The same goes when asking for help or feedback. You don’t need to exhaust every option before talking to a teammate, but you should make a reasonable attempt to understand the problem first, using AI where it helps. Explaining what you’ve tried and where you’re stuck gives them a useful starting point, rather than asking them to do the investigation you skipped.

If you are asking for human attention, demonstrate human effort.
